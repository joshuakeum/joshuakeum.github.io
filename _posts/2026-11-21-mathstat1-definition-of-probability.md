## Terms

- **Random experiment:** an experiment whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty.
- **Sample space** $$\mathcal{C}$$: the set of all possible outcomes of the experiment.
- **Event:** a subset of the sample space.
- **Probability of an event $$C$$.**
  - *Frequentist* — the relative frequency of the event $$C$$. $$\hookrightarrow$$ Interpret probability as the limit of the frequency. (e.g., the probability of the same thing within 50 years — a case where the experiment can be done only once: the definition of probability fails.)
  - *Subjective* — the degree of subjective belief in the event $$C$$.

  $$\rightarrow$$ Statistics starts by defining that there is a probability that everyone agrees on.

**Examples.** Coin toss: $$\mathcal{C} = \{H, T\}$$. Die roll: $$\mathcal{C} = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$$.

## 1.1 $$\sigma$$-fields

> **Definition ($$\sigma$$-field).** For a collection $$\mathcal{B}$$ of subsets of the sample space $$\mathcal{C}$$, $$\mathcal{B}$$ is called a $$\sigma$$-field if it satisfies the following conditions.
>
> - (i) $$\phi \in \mathcal{B}$$
> - (ii) (closed under complements) If $$C \in \mathcal{B}$$, then $$C^{c} \in \mathcal{B}$$.
> - (iii) (closed under countable unions) If $$C_1, C_2, \dots \in \mathcal{B}$$, then $$\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} C_i \in \mathcal{B}$$.
{: .definition #def-sigma-field }

$$\Downarrow$$ **Background of how it arose: Hausdorff's Paradox.**
Think of a set $$[0,1]$$. Hausdorff's paradox says that there exists a subset of $$[0,1]$$ whose 'length' cannot be measured. If we define the length of the subset of $$[0,1]$$ as its probability, there exists a subset whose probability cannot be defined. $$\Rightarrow$$ $$\sigma$$-field needs to be defined.

> **Theorem (Properties of a $$\sigma$$-field $$\mathcal{B}$$ and the sample space $$\mathcal{C}$$).**
>
> - ① $$\mathcal{C} \in \mathcal{B}$$.
> - ② (Closed to countable intersections) $$C_1, \dots \in \mathcal{B} \Rightarrow \bigcap_i C_i \in \mathcal{B}$$.
> - ③ Let $$\mathcal{B}_t$$ ($$t \in T$$) be a $$\sigma$$-field. $$\Rightarrow$$ $$\bigcap_{t \in T} \mathcal{B}_t$$ is also a $$\sigma$$-field.
{: .theorem #thm-sigma-field-properties }

*Proof of ②.* Let $$C_1, \dots \in \mathcal{B}$$ and we will prove that $$\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty} C_i \in \mathcal{B}$$. As $$\mathcal{B}$$ is closed to complements, it is sufficient to prove $$\bigl(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty} C_i\bigr)^{c} \in \mathcal{B}$$. Here, $$\bigl(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty} C_i\bigr)^{c} = \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} C_i^{c} \in \mathcal{B}$$, since $$C_i^{c} \in \mathcal{B}$$ and $$\mathcal{B}$$ is closed to countable unions.

**Examples.** Let $$\mathcal{C}$$ be the sample space.

1. $$\{\phi, \mathcal{C}\}$$: a $$\sigma$$-field.
2. If $$C \subseteq \mathcal{C}$$, $$\{\phi, C, C^{c}, \mathcal{C}\}$$: a $$\sigma$$-field.
3. The power set of $$\mathcal{C}$$: a $$\sigma$$-field. $$\hookrightarrow$$ the set of all subsets of $$\mathcal{C}$$.
4. $$\mathbb{D}$$: some collection of subsets of $$\mathcal{C}$$.

   $$
   \sigma(\mathbb{D}) := \bigcap \{\mathcal{E} : \mathbb{D} \subseteq \mathcal{E},\ \mathcal{E} \text{ is a collection of subsets of } \mathcal{C} \text{ that is a } \sigma\text{-field}\}
   $$

   $$\rightarrow$$ $$\sigma(\mathbb{D})$$ is the $$\sigma$$-field generated by $$\mathbb{D}$$, and the smallest $$\sigma$$-field containing $$\mathbb{D}$$.
5. $$\mathcal{C}$$: a metric space, a topological space.

   $$
   \mathbb{B} = \sigma(\text{open subsets of } \mathcal{C}) : \text{the Borel } \sigma\text{-field.}
   $$

   $$\hookrightarrow$$ The elements of $$\mathbb{B}$$: Borel sets.

## 1.2 Probability

> **Definition (Probability).** Let $$\mathcal{C}$$ be the sample space and $$\mathbb{B}$$ be its $$\sigma$$-field. For a function $$P : \mathbb{B} \to \mathbb{R}$$, define $$P$$ as the *probability* / *probability set function* / *probability measure* if it satisfies the followings.
>
> - (1) $$P(C) \ge 0$$ for all $$C \in \mathbb{B}$$.
> - (2) $$P(\mathcal{C}) = 1$$.
> - (3) (Countable additivity) For $$C_1, C_2, \dots \in \mathbb{B}$$, let $$C_i \cap C_j = \phi$$ if $$i \ne j$$. Then $$P\bigl(\bigcup_{n} C_n\bigr) = \sum_{n} P(C_n)$$.
{: .definition #def-probability }

(cf) Without condition (2), $$P$$ is defined as a *measure*. $$\hookrightarrow$$ A probability is a special case of a measure.

> **Theorem (Properties of probability).**
>
> - (1) $$P(\phi) = 0$$.
> - (2) (Finite additivity) Let $$C_1, C_2 \in \mathbb{B}$$ and $$C_1 \cap C_2 = \phi$$. Then $$P(C_1 \cup C_2) = P(C_1) + P(C_2)$$.
> - (3) $$P(C^{c}) = 1 - P(C)$$.
> - (4) $$0 \le P(C) \le 1$$ for all $$C \in \mathbb{B}$$.
> - (5) (Monotonicity) If $$C_1 \subseteq C_2$$, then $$P(C_1) \le P(C_2)$$.
> - (6) (Countable subadditivity) $$P\bigl(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} C_n\bigr) \le \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} P(C_n)$$ for any $$C_1, \dots \in \mathbb{B}$$.
> - (7) (i) (Continuity from below) If $$C_1 \subseteq C_2 \subseteq \cdots$$, then $$P\bigl(\lim_{n \to \infty} C_n\bigr) = P\bigl(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} C_n\bigr) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(C_n)$$.
>   (ii) (Continuity from above) If $$A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots$$, then $$P\bigl(\lim_{n \to \infty} A_n\bigr) = P\bigl(\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n\bigr) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n)$$.
{: .theorem #thm-probability-properties }

*Proof.* (1) $$\phi = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} \phi$$. Therefore $$P(\phi) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} P(\phi)$$, and so $$P(\phi) = 0$$.

$$\rightarrow$$ Since the probability of a complement has not been proved yet, one must not say $$P(\phi) = 1 - P(\mathcal{C})$$.

*Proof (continued).* (2) Take $$C_3 = \phi = C_4 = \cdots$$. Then
$$P\bigl(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} C_n\bigr) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} P(C_n) = P(C_1) + P(C_2)$$, and the left side is $$P(C_1 \cup C_2)$$.

(5) $$C_2 = C_1 \cup (C_2 \setminus C_1)$$. $$\therefore\ P(C_2) = P(C_1) + P(C_2 \setminus C_1) \ge P(C_1)$$.

(6) Let $$A_1 = C_1$$, $$A_2 = C_2 \setminus C_1$$, $$A_3 = C_3 \setminus (C_1 \cup C_2)$$, $$\dots$$

$$
\therefore\ P\Bigl(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} C_n\Bigr) = P\Bigl(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n\Bigr) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} P(A_n) \le \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} P(C_n).
$$

(7) **Important** — continuity of a function: $$f(\lim x_n) = \lim f(x_n)$$ holds! The proof is done like (6).

**Proofs of properties (3), (4) and (7)** of the theorem on the properties of probability.

*Proof.* (3) $$\mathcal{C} = C \cup C^{c}$$ and $$C \cap C^{c} = \phi$$, so by finite additivity $$1 = P(\mathcal{C}) = P(C) + P(C^{c})$$.

(4) $$P(C) \ge 0$$ by axiom (1), and $$C \subseteq \mathcal{C}$$ gives $$P(C) \le P(\mathcal{C}) = 1$$ by monotonicity (5).

(7)(i) Suppose $$C_1 \subseteq C_2 \subseteq \cdots$$. Put $$D_1 = C_1$$ and $$D_n = C_n \setminus C_{n-1}$$ ($$n \ge 2$$). The $$D_n$$ are disjoint, $$\bigcup_{k=1}^{n} D_k = C_n$$ and $$\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} D_n = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} C_n$$. By countable additivity,

$$
P\Bigl(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} C_n\Bigr) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} P(D_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} P(D_k) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(C_n).
$$

(ii) Suppose $$A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots$$ and put $$C_n = A_n^{c}$$. Then $$C_1 \subseteq C_2 \subseteq \cdots$$, and $$\bigcap_{n} A_n = \bigl(\bigcup_{n} C_n\bigr)^{c}$$. By (i) and (3),

$$
P\Bigl(\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n\Bigr) = 1 - \lim_{n \to \infty} P(C_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \bigl(1 - P(C_n)\bigr) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n). \qquad \square
$$

> **Theorem 1.1.2 in the textbook (probability of a union).**
>
> - (a) $$P(A_1 \cup A_2) = P(A_1) + P(A_2) - P(A_1 \cap A_2)$$.
> - (b) $$P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3) = P(A_1) + P(A_2) + P(A_3) - P(A_1 \cap A_2) - P(A_2 \cap A_3) - P(A_3 \cap A_1) + P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3)$$.
> - (c) (inclusion–exclusion) $$P(A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n) = \sum P(A_i) - \sum_{i<j} P(A_i \cap A_j) + \cdots + (-1)^{n-1} P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_n)$$.
> - (d) (countable subadditivity) $$P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots) \le P(A_1) + P(A_2) + \cdots$$.
{: .theorem #thm-union-probability }

(a) follows from the additivity applied to $$A_1 \cup A_2 = (A_1 \setminus A_2) \cup (A_1 \cap A_2) \cup (A_2 \setminus A_1)$$, $$A_1 = (A_1 \setminus A_2) \cup (A_1 \cap A_2)$$ and $$A_2 = (A_2 \setminus A_1) \cup (A_1 \cap A_2)$$. (b) and (c) follow from (a) by mathematical induction; (d) is property (6) above. Proofs of (c) and of Bonferroni's inequality are written out in [Chapter 1 Exercises](/posts/mathstat1-exercises-chapter-1/).

**Example 1.1.1 (Bernoulli trial).** Tossing a fair coin and observing the face: $$\mathcal{C} = \{H, T\}$$, $$P(\{H\}) = P(\{T\}) = \tfrac{1}{2}$$. An experiment with only two possible outcomes, called success $$s$$ and failure $$f$$, is a *Bernoulli trial*: $$\mathcal{C} = \{s, f\}$$, $$P(\{s\}) = p$$, $$P(\{f\}) = 1 - p$$ ($$0 < p < 1$$).

**Example 1.1.3.** Let $$\mathcal{C} = [0,1]$$ and $$P((a,b]) = b - a$$ ($$a < b$$). Then the probability of a single point is, by continuity from above,

$$
P(\{b\}) = P\Bigl(\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} \bigl(b - \tfrac{1}{n},\, b\bigr]\Bigr) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P\bigl(\bigl(b - \tfrac{1}{n},\, b\bigr]\bigr) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0.
$$
